How to Phase a Landscape Lighting Project in Pittsburgh

Plan a complete first lighting phase, identify shared work, compare future estimates and define the nighttime review before expanding a property-wide design.

Journal/Cost and project scope/Phasing a whole-property design

Plan the whole property, then define a complete first phase

Phasing a landscape lighting project means deciding which part to complete now and which work to leave for a later decision. A useful first phase has a clear purpose on its own. It should not depend on an unapproved future phase to make the main route or evening view feel coherent.

Updated November 21, 2026. The sequence below is a planning example, not a Lumascapes installation record, quotation or promise about what a particular property needs.

Write down the outcome before choosing fixtures

Start with the way you use the property after dark. Which entrance do visitors approach? Which steps connect the house and driveway? Where do you sit outside, and what do you see from the room you use most in the evening? Give the designer a short priority list rather than a requested number of lights.

Separate a current problem from a future preference. A difficult-to-read arrival route may be an immediate priority; highlighting a garden feature you plan to plant next year can remain a later option. Walk the route and review the view from both directions before agreeing on the first phase's boundary.

A fictional three-phase example

Imagine a homeowner who wants to improve the front arrival, use a rear patio more often and eventually add lighting to a redesigned garden. The plan might be divided as follows:

  1. First phase: arrival. Define the approach from parking to the front door, including the relevant turns and steps. Record the intended view from the street and inside the entry. Complete and review this area as a usable scene.
  2. Second phase: patio. Revisit the seating layout and the route between the house and patio before agreeing on its lighting. Check how the proposed scene relates to the completed arrival and whether controls need a separate operating schedule.
  3. Later option: garden. Keep the garden design provisional until planting and paths are settled. Review viewpoints and access when that project is ready rather than treating an early sketch as a final fixture order.

The sequence can change. A household that uses a rear entrance every day may prioritize that route instead. The value of a phased plan is the explicit decision about use, dependencies and completion—not a universal rule that every property should begin at the front.

Identify shared work without assuming spare capacity

Ask the designer or installer to explain which parts of the proposed system are shared across phases and which decisions can wait. Document the proposed control zones, service access and any work that would be repeated if later phases were added independently. Capacity and compatibility need to be checked against the actual design; a statement that a system is “expandable” is not a complete expansion plan.

Distinguish a reservation from installed equipment. For example, a drawing might identify a possible future route without including that future work in the present scope. The proposal should say what is being provided now, what remains only a design allowance and what must be reassessed before a later installation.

Compare the cost of each phase and the whole plan

Request a clearly defined first-phase scope with its own inclusions, exclusions and adjustment process. If the proposal describes later options, ask whether their prices are current estimates or a commitment, what assumptions they use and when they would need to be updated. Do not add tentative future estimates together and treat the result as a fixed whole-property quote.

Ask whether returning for later phases would repeat mobilization, access or restoration work, and what an integrated installation would change. This does not establish that completing everything at once is cheaper or better; it gives you the information needed to compare timing, disruption and the scope you can approve now. See the installation cost guide and quote comparison checklist for the wider questions to include.

Define how the first phase will be accepted

Before work begins, agree on the areas and viewpoints to review after dark. Include the arrival direction, the view back toward the house, the main indoor viewpoint and nearby seating or neighboring windows where relevant. Record what needs adjustment and who will follow up. A drawing marked complete is different from reviewing the installed scene in the conditions in which you will use it.

Keep the accepted plan, equipment information and control settings together with a note about later options. When another phase is considered, begin with that record and any changes to planting, paths or household use. That makes the next conversation a continuation of the actual property plan rather than a new fixture-count exercise.
